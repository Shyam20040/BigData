1Service Level Agreements (SLA)

DefinitionA formal contract between a Service Provider (SP) and a Service Consumer (SC). It is the foundation of the consumer's trust in the provider and defines a formal basis for the performance and availability the SP guarantees. An SLA contains Service Level Objectives (SLOs): objectively measurable conditions for the service. SLA and SLO are the basis for selecting a cloud provider.

SLA contents

  • The set of services the provider will deliver, each completely and specifically defined.
  • Responsibilities of the provider and the consumer.
  • Metrics to measure whether services are delivered as guaranteed, and an auditing mechanism to monitor them.
  • Remedies available to the consumer and provider if terms are not met; how the SLA will change over time.

Types of SLA and SLOs

  • Off-the-shelf (non-negotiable / direct) SLA: the provider creates the template and defines all criteria (contract period, billing, response time, availability); followed by today's state-of-the-art clouds; not suitable for mission-critical data or applications.
  • Negotiable SLA: negotiation via an external agent, or via multiple external agents.
  • SLOs are objectively measurable and cover QoS parameters such as availability, serviceability, billing, penalties, throughput, response time or quality. Examples: "availability of service X is 99.9%"; "response time of database query Q is between 3 and 5 seconds"; "throughput of server S at peak load is 0.875".
  • Service level management: monitoring and measuring performance against SLOs. Provider view: decisions based on business objectives and technical realities; consumer view: decisions on how to use cloud services.

2KPIs, Metrics, Examples & Limitations

Key Performance Indicators (KPIs): low-level resource metrics (downtime, uptime, in-bytes, out-bytes, packet size…) that are composed, aggregated or converted into high-level SLOs. Example mapping: availability (A) is derived from downtime and uptime.

Monitoring: who should monitor the provider, and does the consumer meet its responsibilities? A neutral third party removes conflicts of interest (the provider reporting outages at its sole discretion; the consumer responsible for an outage). Auditability: is the provider adhering to legal regulations or industry standards; the SLA should state how and when audits happen.

Limitations of current SLAsService measurement is restricted to uptime percentage (mean availability over a period), ignoring stability, capacity, etc. Bias towards vendors: parameters set to the vendor's advantage. Lack of active monitoring by the customer: only ticketing systems; no access to active data streams or audit trails, no outage reports from providers. Gap between QoS hype and reality: governance, reliability, availability, security and scalability poorly addressed; no formal way to verify compliance.
Why proper SLAs matterProvider: improves infrastructure and enables fair competition. Customer: builds trust and supports choosing an appropriate provider to move business to the cloud.

3Cloud Economics: Common Infrastructure & Location Independence

Cloud propertyEconomic meaning
Common infrastructurePooled, standardized resources; benefits from statistical multiplexing
Location independenceUbiquitous availability meeting performance needs; benefits from latency reduction and better user experience
Online connectivityEnabler of the other attributes; network cost and performance can be quantified by traditional methods
Utility pricingUsage-sensitive, pay-per-use pricing; benefits in on-demand environments
On-demand resourcesScalable, elastic resources provisioned and de-provisioned without delay or cost

Value of common infrastructure

  • Economies of scale: reduced overhead costs; buyer power through volume purchasing.
  • Statistics of scale: for infrastructure built to peak requirements, multiplexing demand gives higher utilization and lower cost per delivered resource; for infrastructure built to less than peak, multiplexing reduces unserved demand, so less lost revenue or SLA-violation payout.
Coefficient of variation (a measure of "smoothness")The ratio of the standard deviation σ to the absolute value of the mean (not the variance, nor the correlation coefficient). It compares variation across data series even when their means differ greatly.
Cv=σ|μ|
"Smoother" curves have a large mean for a given σ, or a smaller σ for a given mean. A facility with fixed assets serving highly variable demand achieves lower utilization than one serving smooth demand, and multiplexing demand from several sources may reduce Cv. (In investing, a lower σ-to-mean ratio means a better risk–return trade-off.)

Aggregating independent demands

Take n independent random demands X1, X2, …, Xn, each with identical mean μ and standard deviation σ. The aggregate has mean equal to the sum of means and variance equal to the sum of variances:

Mean=n·μ  , Variance=n·σ2Cv(n)=n·σ2n·μ=σn·μ=1n·Cv
ResultAdding n independent demands reduces Cv by a factor of 1/√n, so the penalty of insufficient or excess resources grows smaller. Aggregating 100 workloads brings the penalty to 10% (1/√100 = 0.1).

But what about real workloads?

  • Negatively correlated demands: X and 1 − X; their sum is the constant 1, so choosing customer segments appropriately removes variability.
  • Perfectly correlated demands (simultaneous peaks): aggregation gives no benefit; Cv stays constant.
Aggregate=n·X,mean=nμ,std. dev.=nσ⇒Cv=nσnμ=σμ
  • In the real world: with correlated demands, private, mid-size and large providers see similar statistics of scale; with independent demands, mid-size providers achieve statistical economies similar to an infinitely large provider. Data on economies of scale for large providers is mixed (same COTS components, cheap power locations, early automation tools, third parties taking care of operations).

Value of location independence

Applications, services and content now come to us over wired, wireless and satellite networks. Latency: human response latency is tens to hundreds of ms; latency correlates strongly with distance (routing algorithms are second-order); light in fibre travels only about 124 miles per ms. Examples: Google word suggestion taking 2 s would be unusable; VoIP degrades at 200 ms or more. Serving a global user base requires a dispersed service architecture (coordination, consistency, availability, partition tolerance) with investment implications.

4Utility Pricing & Provisioning Penalty

Economy of scale may not be very effective, but cloud services need not be cheaper per unit to be economical. Car analogy: buying or leasing costs INR 10,000 per day, renting costs INR 45,000 per day; for a 2-day trip renting is far cheaper than owning, so it depends on demand.

Utility pricing in detailLet demand be D(t) for 0 < t < T, with peak P and average A. A baseline (owned) system must be provisioned for the peak; the utility is charged per use at a utility premium U (utility cost per unit divided by baseline cost per unit, e.g. 45,000/10,000 = 4.5 in the car example).
BT=P×TCT=∫0TU×D(t)dt=U×A×TCT<BT⇒U×A×T<P×T⇒U<PA
The utility is cheaper whenever the premium is below the peak-to-average ratio. Spiky, highly variable demand (large P/A) favours the cloud even at a high U.

Penalty cost

Penalty = either paying for unused resources or missing service delivery, i.e. the area between demand D(t) and the provisioned resource R(t). If resources follow demand with provisioning delay tp, demand that grows exponentially falls exponentially behind:

D(t)=et ,  R(t)=et−tpPenalty=∫|D(t)−R(t)|dtD(t)−R(t)=et−et−tp=et(1−e−tp)=k1et

The penalty grows in proportion to the demand itself: with exponential demand, any fixed provisioning interval according to current demand falls exponentially behind, so the penalty cost keeps growing even with continuous monitoring and a non-zero provisioning interval.

5Managing Data: Relational and Parallel Databases

Relational databases have been the default storage and retrieval mechanism since the 1980s (System R, Ingres), efficient for transaction processing, replacing hierarchical and network databases. For scalable web search Google built GFS (massively parallel, fault-tolerant distributed file system), BigTable (data organization, similar to column-oriented databases such as Vertica) and MapReduce (parallel programming paradigm). These suit large-volume, massively parallel text processing and enterprise analytics; similar to the BigTable model are Google App Engine's Datastore and Amazon's SimpleDB.

RDBMS internals

  • Query layer: users interact via SQL; the parser transforms queries into memory and disk-level operations and optimizes execution time.
  • Disk-space management: records stored on pages of contiguous memory blocks, fetched with pre-fetching and page-replacement policies.
  • Database file system layer: independent of the OS file system, to keep full control over retaining or releasing a page in memory; files may span multiple disks and use parallel I/O (RAID arrays, multiprocessor clusters).
Storage techniqueRow-orientedColumn-oriented
Best forWrite-oriented operations, transaction processingData-warehouse workloads
LayoutRelational records on contiguous disk pages; accessed via primary index on chosen columns (e.g. B+-tree)Projection of a table stored sorted by dimension values; aggregation of measure columns by dimension values
IndexingIndex → records on pages of rowsNeeds multiple join indexes if different projections must be indexed in sorted order

Parallel database architectures

ArchitectureDescription
Shared memoryServers with multiple CPUs; memory address space shared and managed by a symmetric multiprocessing (SMP) OS, which schedules processes in parallel across all processors
Shared diskHybrid: independent server clusters share storage over a high-speed network (NAS or SAN) via Ethernet, faster Fibre Channel or InfiniBand
Shared nothingCluster of independent servers, each with its own disk space, connected by a network

Advantages of parallel DB over a plain relational DB: efficient SQL execution by exploiting multiple processors; in shared-nothing, tables are partitioned across nodes, the SQL optimizer handles distributed joins, and distributed two-phase commit locking isolates transactions; fault tolerant (control transferred to a stand-by system for transaction processing, or computations restored for data warehousing). Examples: transaction processing: Oracle, DB2, SQL Server; data warehousing: Netezza, Vertica, Teradata.

Parallel computing models: shared-memory (any processor can access any memory location; unequal latency) and distributed-memory (each processor accesses only its own memory and uses message passing). Parallel computing began with compute-intensive scientific tasks and moved into databases.

6Parallel Efficiency (Worked Illustration)

Parallel efficiencyIf a task takes time T on a uniprocessor, it should ideally take T/p on p processors. Inefficiencies arise from synchronization among processors, message-communication overheads and imbalance in work distribution. Parallel efficiency is
ε=Tp·Tp
A scalable parallel implementation keeps efficiency constant as data size and processor count grow together, and efficiency increases with data size for a fixed number of processors.

Problem: given n documents (e.g. crawled web pages) and m distinct words, compute the frequency (total occurrences) of each word, i.e. m frequencies. Assumptions: distributed-memory model with a shared disk (no contention); each word occurs f times per document on average; time to read a word = time to send it = c; addition time negligible.

Approach 1: split the wordsEach processor computes frequencies for m/p words across all documents, so it reads every word of every document (wasted reads).
T=n×m×f×c ,   Tp=n×m×f×cεa=nmfcp·nmfc=1p
Efficiency falls with increasing p: not scalable.
Approach 2: split the documentsEach processor computes frequencies of all m words over n/p documents, then processors sum their results.
Tp=(np)mfc+cm+p×(mp)×cεb=nmfcp·Tp=nmfcnmfc+2pcm=nfnf+2p=11+2pnf≈1  for p ≪ nf
Every read is useful; efficiency is constant as n and p grow proportionally and tends to 1 for fixed p as n grows: scalable.

7Cloud File Systems and Data Stores

GFS / HDFS

  • GFS: manages relatively large files on a very large cluster of commodity servers on a high-speed network; handles failures even during reads/writes; supports parallel reads, writes and appends by many clients. Fault tolerance is a necessity because the chance that something fails grows with the number of components N:
p(system failure)=1−(1−p(component failure))N→1   (for large N)
  • HDFS: open-source implementation of the GFS architecture, available on Amazon EC2.
  • Architecture: a single master (GFS) / NameNode (HDFS) controls the file namespace; files split into chunks (GFS) / blocks (HDFS), typically 64 MB, stored on chunk servers / DataNodes (commodity Linux servers), replicated three times across different physical racks and network segments.
OperationSteps
ReadClient sends file path and offset to the master → master returns metadata for one replica of the chunk → client caches the metadata → reads from the chunk server.
Write / appendClient sends path to master → master returns metadata for all replicas → client sends data to all chunk servers, which acknowledge → master designates one as primary → primary appends at an offset it chooses (can be beyond EOF to handle multiple writers) and sends the offset to replicas → if any replica fails, the primary retries.
Fault toleranceMaster exchanges heartbeat messages with chunk servers; on failure the metadata is updated, a new primary is assigned if the primary failed, and clients refresh metadata from the master and retry.

BigTable

A distributed structured storage system built on GFS: a sparse, persistent, multi-dimensional sorted map of key–value pairs accessed by row key, column key and timestamp (example row com.cnn.www with columns contents: and anchor:cnnsi.com).

  • Columns have the form column-family : label; the set of column families is fixed when the table is created, but labels can be added dynamically at any time. Each cell stores multiple versions in decreasing timestamp order; data in a column is stored together for efficient access.
  • Storage: each table is split into row ranges called tablets, each managed by a tablet server, which stores each column family of its row range in a separate distributed file, an SSTable (HBase: HStore). A single metadata table (managed by a metadata server) locates the tablets for any read/write; it can itself be split into tablets, with a root tablet pointing to the other metadata tablets. Supports large parallel reads and inserts, even simultaneously; insertions are sorted, so they need more work than a simple append.

Dynamo (Amazon)

  • Supports a large volume of concurrent, small updates (unlike BigTable, which targets bulk reads/writes); data model is a simple <key, value> pair, well suited to e-commerce; independent of any distributed file system for failure handling, replication, forwarding writes if a replica is down, and conflict resolution.
  • Ring architecture: objects are arbitrary byte arrays; MD5 generates a 128-bit hash whose range maps onto virtual nodes arranged in a ring; each key maps to one virtual node and is replicated at a primary and additional virtual nodes; each physical node manages several virtual nodes at distributed ring positions (load balancing for transient failures and network partitions).
  • Writes: executed at one virtual node and forwarded to all replica nodes; a quorum protocol maintains eventual consistency under many concurrent reads and writes.
  • Versioning: each write creates a new version with an incremented local (vector) timestamp capturing update history; superseded versions are discarded; concurrent writes keep all versions, returned to readers, and conflicts are resolved by application-independent logic.
Quorum consistencyA read accesses R replicas and a write accesses W replicas out of N. The system is quorum consistent if
(R+W)>N
Trade-off: for efficient writes, more replicas must be read; for efficient reads, more replicas must be written. Dynamo's node-level storage engines include Berkeley DB (used by Amazon) and MySQL.

Datastore (Google App Engine) and SimpleDB

  • Simple transactional <key, value> stores. All Datastore entities live in one BigTable table (no column-oriented storage; the entities table keeps data as one column family), sharded across disks and sorted lexicographically by key, enabling efficient prefix and range queries. Entities are grouped for transactions (keys lexicographic by group ancestry; same group stored close together).
  • Index tables (using entity attribute values as keys) support varied queries: automatic single-property indexes (WHERE-clause lookups) and kind indexes (SELECT ALL queries), plus configurable composite indexes for complex queries. The query executor picks the index with the highest selectivity.

8MapReduce

MapReduce is Google's programming model for large-scale search and text processing on web data stored in BigTable/GFS: massively parallel computation across tens of thousands of processors, fault tolerant so progress continues despite processor or network failures. Hadoop is the open-source implementation (developed at Yahoo!), available pre-packaged on Amazon EC2.

The model

A parallel programming abstraction over a common fault-tolerant implementation, with two phases (map and reduce). A configurable number M of mapper and R of reducer processors work on a problem, coordinated by a single master.

Map:  (k1,v1)→[(k2,v2)]Reduce:  (k2,[v2])→(k2,f([v2]))
Map phaseEach mapper reads about 1/M of the input from the global file system (locations from the master), transforms key–value pairs, and writes one file per reducer, sorted by key, to its local file system. The master tracks file locations.
→
Reduce phaseThe master tells reducers where the files are. Reducers fetch them by remote procedure call, group by key, apply f to each value list, and write results back to GFS.

Word-count example (M = 3 mappers, R = 2 reducers; documents d1…d10 with words w1…w4): the map function emits a count per word per document, the reduce function sums the counts for each word.

Map:  (dk,[w1…wn])→[(wi,ci)]Reduce:  (wi,[ci])→(wi,∑ici)

Fault tolerance: heartbeat messages report task status. If a worker communicates but makes no progress, the master duplicates its tasks on processors that have finished. If a mapper fails, its key range is reassigned and re-executed, because partial results sit on local files, not GFS. If a reducer fails, only its remaining tasks are reassigned, since completed work is already in GFS.

Efficiency of MapReduce

A task on data volume D takes wD on a uniprocessor (read + compute + write back; c = time to read/write one word). Decomposed into map and reduce stages with no decomposition overhead:

wD=cD+cmD+crσD+cσμDMap time=cmD , map output=σD , reduce time=crσD , reduce output=σμD

With P processors acting as mappers and then reducers, the extra overhead is each mapper writing to local disk and each reducer reading remotely from every mapper (reading a word locally or remotely costs the same):

Per mapper:  read=cDP,  map output=σDP,  local write=cσDPEach reducer reads σDP2 from each of P mappers (reducer r reads mapper (r + i) mod P in step i)Transfer per reducer=P×cσDP2=cσDP,  overhead=2cσDP

Parallel efficiency of the MapReduce implementation:

εMR=wDP(wDP+2cσDP)=ww+2cσ=11+2cσw

Overhead is small when the computation cost per word w is large relative to 2cσ, so efficiency approaches 1 for compute-heavy tasks.

Applications

  • Indexing a large collection of documents (key to web search and structured data): map emits a (word, document-id) pair for each word; reduce groups pairs by word and builds an index entry.
Map:  (dk,[w1…wn])→[(wv,dk)]Reduce:  [(wi,dk)]→(wi,[di1…dim])
  • Relational operations (SQL joins, group by) on large data sets, with advantages over parallel databases in scale and fault tolerance.

9OpenStack

DefinitionOpenStack is a cloud operating system that controls large pools of compute, storage and networking resources across a datacenter, managed through a dashboard that gives administrators control while letting users provision resources through a web interface. It began as a collaboration between NASA and Rackspace; in 2017 job-posting trends showed OpenStack ahead of EC2, CloudStack and Eucalyptus.

Capabilities: IaaS (provision compute, network, storage), with PaaS (e.g. Cloud Foundry) built on top and SaaS via browser or thin client; VMs on demand, provisioning, snapshotting, networking, storage for VMs and arbitrary files, multi-tenancy, quotas for projects and users (a user can belong to several projects). Releases run Austin (2010) to Ocata (2017, latest in slides), then Pike and Queens under development.

10Quick Revision

Must remember
  • SLA: contract between provider and consumer; contains SLOs; off-the-shelf SLAs are non-negotiable and unsuited to mission-critical use; WS-Agreement and WSLA for web services; neutral third-party monitoring; limits: uptime-only metrics, vendor bias, weak consumer monitoring.
  • Economics: Cv = σ/|μ|; aggregating n independent demands gives Cv/√n (100 workloads → 10%); correlated demands give no gain; utility is cheaper when U < P/A; exponential demand with provisioning delay tp gives penalty ∝ et.
  • Parallel efficiency: ε = T/(p·Tp); approach 1 (split words) has ε = 1/p; approach 2 (split documents) has ε = nf/(nf + 2p) ≈ 1 for p ≪ nf.
  • Parallel DB: shared memory, shared disk, shared nothing. GFS/HDFS: single master, 64 MB chunks, 3 replicas, primary-based append. BigTable: row/column/timestamp, tablets, SSTables. Dynamo: key–value, MD5 ring, quorum R + W > N, vector timestamps. Datastore: single BigTable table with index tables.
  • MapReduce: map (k1,v1) → [(k2,v2)]; reduce (k2,[v2]) → (k2, f([v2])); mapper failure ⇒ re-execute (local files), reducer failure ⇒ only remaining tasks; εMR = 1/(1 + 2σc/w).
  • OpenStack: Nova, Neutron, Swift, Cinder, Keystone, Glance, Ceilometer, Horizon.
